\noindent\textbf{NEVERMIND. Start with one moving link.}

The first commit jumped straight to assigning beams in a frozen scene. That assumes I already know when a beam can serve a target. I'd rather work that out for one moving satellite first. One satellite, one ground target, one pass: orbit, where to point, when the signal arrives, and what Doppler does to its frequency. I also tried one nearby interfering beam to see what a toy SINR looks like.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{[done-ish] Motion first.} Keep it in a plane for now. Let $r=(x,y)$ and $v=(v_x,v_y)$ for the satellite:
\[
\dot r=v,\qquad \dot v=-\frac{\mu r}{\lVert r\rVert^3}.
\]
Start on a circular orbit at $R+h$ with speed $\sqrt{\mu/(R+h)}$; take 1-second RK4 steps in C++. The code compares the final numerical position with the circular-orbit position at the same time. Ground target rotates:
\[
 g(t)=R(\cos\omega_E t,\sin\omega_E t).
\]
All the calculation uses metres, seconds, radians. I picked $h=550$ km and $f=2$ GHz just to get numbers out.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{[done] Aim + arrival.} A signal sent from $r(t)$ has to meet the target at $g(t+\tau)$, not $g(t)$. I repeat this equation a few times to get $\tau$:
\[
\tau=\frac{\lVert g(t+\tau)-r(t)\rVert}{c},\qquad
 a=\frac{g(t+\tau)-r(t)}{\lVert g(t+\tau)-r(t)\rVert}.
\]
$a$ is the direction to aim; the code calculates it but does not draw a beam. It also measures how far the target moves if I aim at its old position $g(t)$; that output is \texttt{no\_lead\_m}. The \texttt{visible} column means elevation is at least $10^\circ$ above the target's horizon. This is a point/ray toy model; I have not given the beam a width.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{[done] Doppler.} Satellite and target both move. In this model, with $a$ pointing along the ray,
\[
\frac{f_{\rm received}}{f_{\rm sent}}=
\frac{c-a\cdot v_g(t+\tau)}{c-a\cdot v_s(t)}.
\]
The output shows the shift if I send 2 GHz, plus the transmit adjustment needed to get 2 GHz at the target. Approaching gives a higher received frequency; receding gives a lower one.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Numbers so far:} directly overhead: 550 km range, 1.835 ms travel, about 0.85 m of ground motion during that time. At $t=-120$ s the shift is about $+37.5$ kHz; at $t=+120$ s it is about $-37.5$ kHz. Small aiming miss, noticeable frequency shift. The size of that miss relative to a beam footprint comes later.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{[added] Beam contour (?) + SINR.} I put a second ground target $0.3^\circ$ away and point another beam from the same satellite at it. Both beams use the same frequency and 10 W. I am pretending the transmit gain falls off like
\[
G(\theta)=1000\exp\!\left[-\frac12\left(\frac{\theta}{2^\circ}\right)^2\right].
\]
The receiver gain is 1. Same free-space path factor for both beams at the first target; noise is $kTB$ with $T=290$ K and $B=1$ MHz:
\[
S=10G(0)\left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi d}\right)^2,\quad
I=10G(\theta)\left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi d}\right)^2,\quad
\mathrm{SINR}=\frac{S}{I+kTB}.
\]
At overhead I get about 6.5 dB SINR. Near the edge of this pass it is around 0 dB. That is what this made-up contour says, not an antenna measurement. The code re-aims both beams at every sample, so there is no stale-pointing effect yet.
